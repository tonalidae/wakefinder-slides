What is the Milky Way made of?
Dark matter properties and clues about models of the universe.
  • 85% of the mass in the universe
  • Emits no electromagnetic radiation ; known only through gravity
  • About 5× more abundant than visible matter
  • Evidence: rotation curves, gravitational lensing, CMB anisotropy
  • Without it structure could not have formed at all
Dark matter is about 85% of the mass in the universe. It emits no electromagnetic radiation, so we know it only through gravity. Evidence: missing mass in galaxy rotation curves (~5x the visible matter), gravitational lensing, and anisotropy patterns in the CMB. It is also needed for structure to form at all — baryonic matter alone was too hot to collapse under gravity in the early universe. Different DM models perturb halos differently, which is what makes this kind of analysis a way to tell models apart.
Dynamical friction
The mechanism is resonance. The LMC's orbital frequency aligns with the natural frequencies of halo particles, which get trapped in resonant orbits sharing similar velocities. That builds the density wake trailing the satellite. The wake then pulls back on the LMC — dynamical friction (Chandrasekhar 1943) — draining its kinetic energy so the orbit decays and circularises. The drag is stronger for more massive satellites, which is why a 1:10 mass ratio matters here.
Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC)

~1/10 of the Milky Way's mass · ~50 kpc away past pericentre · moving at 327 km/s · on first infall

Numbers worth saying out loud: the LMC is the most massive satellite of the Milky Way, roughly one tenth of the host mass. It is about 50 kpc away, just past pericentre, moving at about 327 km/s. Evidence points to a first infall. If it had completed several orbits, the wake would be washed out by destructive interference and we would not be able to see it — so its detectability is itself an argument for first infall.

Tentative observations of the dark matter wake

Gaia + WISE, low-metallicity stars in optical and infrared. Several orbits would wash the wake out by destructive interference.

Ref: Conroy, Naidu, Garavito-Camargo et al. (2021)

Conroy et al. measured variations in stellar density that matched the predicted location of the dark matter wake, evidence that the MW halo is out of equilibrium. They used Gaia and WISE, measuring low-metallicity stars in both optical and infrared to get distances and proper motions. Stars and dark matter particles feel the same dynamical response, which is why a stellar overdensity traces the dark matter wake.

Data analysis

  • N-body simulations run with Gadget-3 (Garavito-Camargo et al. 2021)
  • DM particle mass resolution 4×104 M☉
  • We analyse 106 particles beyond 50 kpc from the halo centre
    • The simulations are Garavito-Camargo et al. (2021), run with Gadget-3: eight high-resolution runs testing how the MW halo responds to different DM models. Ours is collisionless Cold Dark Matter. MW halo mass 1.2e12 solar masses, initial LMC halo 1.8e11 — the 1:10 ratio that makes dynamical friction relevant. The 50 kpc floor matters: those particles are low density and far enough from the potential's centre to actually respond to the LMC, which makes asymmetries visible.

What we compute
  • Data: 3D positions and velocities with their id

  • Classification into low and high angular momentum regions
Physical intuition: does angular momentum spatially delimit the wake?
\[\begin{aligned} \vec{L} = \vec{r} \times \vec{v} \end{aligned} \]
First, rotate into the LMC orbital plane

The orbit is contained in 0.987x + 0.112y − 0.114z = 0, so it lies in XY. Every projection that follows is in these rotated coordinates.

In the raw simulation the LMC's passages run mostly along the x axis. Rotating the whole system into the orbital plane puts the orbit in XY, so the largest changes show up in one projection instead of being smeared across three. Left panel: the orbit has positive orientation. Right: it is fully contained in the plane.
How the wake is selected

Cut 1 — in the unperturbed halo, keep L < 10 000 kpc km/s

Cut 2 — take those same ids in the perturbed halo and keep the band L1 < L < L2, with r < 125 kpc

The values of L1 and L2 are defined as:

\[\begin{aligned} L_1 &= 220\,(r-50) + 13\,000 \\ L_2 &= 220\,(r-50) + 25\,000 \end{aligned}\]

The 125 kpc limit follows a spike in the distance distribution, followed by a drop in |L|.

One honest caveat to raise here. Because the particles that gain the most angular momentum are the ones near the LMC's orbit, the selected set is asymmetric by construction. That means we cannot apply statistics based on distance from the halo centre — the asymmetry is what we are looking for, not a bias to correct away.

The velocity field aligns with the LMC orbit

Left: isotropic directions. Right: arrows comb into alignment along the orbit (red), and the density concentrates on it.

Quantitatively: the divergence of the velocity field turns positive, and both the standard deviation of the angles and the circular variance drop sharply compared with the unperturbed halo. The spatial scale also shrinks, which is why the perturbed panel looks denser. A natural next step is gradient and curl as geometric filters — wake particles should show a lower gradient because they are more aligned.

What the phase diagram shows
  • Selected particles pile up at positive Lz: they corotate with the LMC, whose orbit lies in the XY plane
  • Energy rises by about 10 000 as seen in galaxy mergers
  • The sharp edge in the red distribution is our own L = 10 000 selection threshold
  • No pre-interaction data needed

The same particles are symmetric about Lx = 0. The offset appears only in Lz the axis the orbit actually lives in.

The selected wake follows the LMC orbit

Wake candidates in 3D, coloured by specific angular momentum. The red curve is the LMC orbit.

This is the whole argument in one picture: the selected particles trail the LMC orbit, and their angular momentum rises along the trail. The robustness checks are on the next slide.
  • Lower first-cut thresholds and higher second-cut intercepts both sharpen the peak
  • Selecting too few particles makes structure disappears
  • The width of the second band makes no difference for particles below the initial cut
  • Random samples give saturated plots with no pattern at all
